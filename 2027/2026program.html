PLENARY SPEAKERS

Shaping the Future of Human-Robot Collaboration
Oussama Khatib
Stanford University

Distancing humans from hazardous, unreachable spaces while leveraging their skills and
intuition is fundamentally transforming remote operations. OceanOneK exemplifies this
paradigm. Serving as a robotic avatar, it allows divers to explore extreme environments from
the safety of a ship. Its autonomous physical interaction skills merge with human cognitive
expertise via an intuitive haptic/stereo-vision interface. Using our OpenSAI architecture, the
robot learns compliant strategies from human demonstrations to perform delicate tasks.
Following rigorous experimental validation, OceanOneK reached a record-breaking depth of
1,000 meters in the Mediterranean, successfully recovering fragile archaeological artifacts.
These breakthroughs in collaborative synergy expand our capacity to deliver medical care,
maintain infrastructure, and conduct disaster recovery-from the deep ocean to outer space.

Biography of Oussama Khatib
Oussama Khatib received his PhD from Sup’Aero, Toulouse, France, in 1980. He is Professor of
Computer Science at Stanford University and Director of the Stanford Robotics Center (SRC). He
is a recipient of an honorary doctorate from the University of Montpellier. His research focuses
on methodologies and technologies in human-centered robotics, haptic interactions, artificial
intelligence, and human motion synthesis. Professor Khatib is President of the International
Foundation of Robotics Research (IFRR) and an IEEE Fellow. He is Editor of the Springer STAR and
SPAR series, Springer Handbook of Robotics, and Springer Encyclopedia of Robotics. He is a
recipient of the IEEE Robotics and Automation, Pioneering Award, the George Saridis Leadership
Award, and the Distinguished Service Award. Professor Khatib is recipient of the Japan Robot
Association (JARA) Award, the Rudolf Kalman Award, and the IEEE Technical Field Award. He is
Knight of the French National Order of Merit and a member of the United Sates National
Academy of Engineering. Professor Khatib is recipient of the 2024 Great Arab Minds Award.

Enhancing autonomy of factory automation systems despite physical uncertainty and faults

Birgit Vogel-Heuser

TUM 

The transformation from intelligent to autonomous factory automation systems requires appropriate hardware and software architectures including digital twins as knowledge base. Nevertheless autonomy is often reduced in case of faults of the physical systems. The talk will introduce dynamic reconfiguration to cover such faults.  Different successful applications will be introduced to showcase the usage and benefit of the architectures as well as enabling technologies like Multi Agent Systems and ontologies representing knowledge in a formal and accessible way.

Biography of Birgit Vogel-Heuser

Birgit Vogel-Heuser received her Dipl. Ing. degree in electrical engineering in 1987 and her Dr.-Ing. degree in mechanical engineering in 1990 from the RWTH Aachen, Germany. Over ten years, she acquired industrial experience in industrial automation. After different professorship positions, she was appointed to the Chair of Automation and Information Systems at the Technical University Munich in 2009. Her research is focussing on evolvable field-level automation and appropriate architectures for manufacturing and logistics systems. She is a Fellow of the IEEE, IEEE RAS Distinguished Lecturer, Co-Chair of IEEE RAS TC Digital Manufacturing and Human-Centered Automation and a member of the National Academy of Science and Engineering in Germany (acatech). She has held the Order of Merit of the Federal Republic of Germany since 2024.

KEYNOTE SPEAKERS

Toward Resilient Autonomy: Intelligent Control for Perception-Driven Navigation

Kashiri Navvab

Leonardo Innovation Hub & IP

The deployment of autonomous robotic systems in real-world environments demands far more than the ability to follow a predefined path. It requires the capacity to perceive, reason, and act reliably in the face of uncertainty; to navigate dynamic, partially known, and potentially adversarial conditions without human intervention. This talk addresses the core challenge of developing such systems. We explore how intelligent control, spanning model predictive control, adaptive and learning-based strategies  can be tightly coupled with rich perceptual representations to produce navigation behaviors that are robust, efficient, and context-aware. Central to this discussion is the role of multi-modal perception, combining vision, depth sensing, and proprioception, in grounding control decisions within an accurate and continuously updated model of the environment. Rather than treating perception and control as sequential pipeline stages, we advocate for their deep integration: a closed-loop architecture in which what the robot senses actively shapes how it moves, and how it moves actively informs what it needs to sense. Together, these threads outline a research trajectory aimed at closing the gap between laboratory-demonstrated autonomy and field-deployable resilience, with implications for applications in defense, critical infrastructure security, and search-and-rescue operations.

Biography of Kashiri Navvab

Navvab Kashiri is Principal Investigator of the Robotics Lab at Leonardo Innovation Hub, where he leads research bridging academia and industry. He received his Ph.D. in Robotics from the Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia (IIT) in 2015, with a focus on the development and control of robotic platforms, emphasizing modularity, energy efficiency, and agility. During his time at IIT, he made key contributions to landmark projects including the CENTAURO hybrid locomotion and manipulation platform – a resilient system engineered for disaster response in unstructured environments. He has since been actively engaged in national and international collaborations advancing robotic systems with enhanced capabilities, supporting Leonardo’s mission to develop next-generation solutions for adaptability and autonomy.

The COMPARE Ecosystem: Improving Reproducibility and Integration of Open-Source for Robot Grasping and Manipulation

Adam Norton

University of Massachusetts Lowell

When developing a novel software component of a robot system, researchers typically integrate open-source code for the remaining components in the pipeline, such as perception, grasp planning, and motion planning. However, there are several bottlenecks related to the accessibility and reproducibility of open-source which are stifling research, development, evaluation, and deployment of robots. To this end, the COMPARE Ecosystem is working to develop community-driven guidelines for modular components in robot software pipelines in the context of grasping and manipulation, specifically with a picking in clutter use case. These guidelines are developed through a 5 step process: (1) Characterize open-source components, (2) Investigate existing modular pipelines, (3) Conduct reproducibility and integration exercises, (4) Develop new modular pipelines, and (5) Glean best practices and lessons learned. In this talk, I will review the architecture and methodology used to date to reproduce and integrate various open-source components into pipelines, highlighting issues encountered along the way, and recommendations for improving accessibility and reproducibility using modular components are presented, including several test and evaluation exercises.

Sensor calibration for enhancing the reliability of autonomous systems

Ivan Petrović

University of Zagreb

Reliable autonomous systems depend critically on precise and resilient sensor calibration, especially as modern platforms integrate heterogeneous perception modalities such as cameras, LiDAR, radar, and inertial sensors. The talk explores intrinsic, extrinsic, and spatiotemporal calibration methods. It also discusses emerging calibration-aware algorithms, online self-calibration strategies, and uncertainty-quantified calibration models that adapt to dynamic conditions and sensor degradation. By linking calibration accuracy to downstream autonomy performance, the keynote underscores that robust, scalable calibration pipelines are fundamental to dependable autonomous operation. The talk will present recent research activities and achievements of the LAMOR lab in sensor calibration, focusing on advanced calibration techniques recently developed and the experimental results validating them. The talk will conclude with a brief discussion of open research challenges and possible directions for future research.

Beyond soft sensing: drawing inspiration from the elephant trunk to create sensitive armor skins and to develop monolithic soft sensitive manipulators

Lucia Beccai

IIT

Deformable, or soft, robots while becoming increasingly sophisticated and dexterous, need to transition from controlled laboratory settings to unstructured real-world environments. They require sensing capabilities like tactile sensing to perceive their surroundings, and their own movements. A wide range of technological approaches are available today for soft tactile sensors and skins – that can sense and adapt to both the hosting robot and the outer world. However, we are still far from achieving fully integrated robotic sensing. As shown by our preliminary benchmarking exercises. Then they also must be rugged against mechanical damage: an evolution to soft ‘armor skins’ is necessary, where deformation, tactile sensitivity, and ruggedness co-exist.

The elephant’s trunk is a remarkable natural a versatile manipulator – a muscular hydrostat that combines delicacy and strength – which inspires roboticists to create new concepts of continuum robots. Its skin is an essential structural element that protects the trunk, supports its movements, and facilitates sensory input. In this talk I will introduce our study on this natural skin model to extract some biological design principles based on its unique hierarchical structure, which deforms, freely accommodating the trunk versatile motions while maintaining mechanical robustness, providing protection while also allowing for highly sensitive touch. I will show a new concept of sensitive armor skin designed and built to exhibit high deformability, resistance to puncture and tear, tactile and strain sensing capability based on optical transduction principle.  

In the last part of my talk, I will show our research towards fully integrated soft robots based on the co-design of soft sensing, actuation and robot body. I will present an elephant-inspired gripper based on a monolithic structure, introducing the need in soft robotics of approaches different from typical system engineering based on monolithic fabrication technologies for future reliable systems.

Why you are all doing AI and why does it matter! Understanding the AI Act and its risk-based categorization

Andrea Bertolini

Scuola Superiore Sant’Anna

The presentation will address the notion of AI System (AIS) and high-risk AIS (h-AIS) that are at the heart of the European regulation on artificial intelligence, namely the AI Act. The purpose is multifold. Firstly, it will explain how almost all technological innovation falls under the notion of AI and is thence subject to the complex regulatory framework emergent at European level. Secondly, it will address the risk-categorization and attempt to clarify and demystify it. The notion of risk adopted by European policymakers does not mirror or resemble the technical one, is not based on measurable and objectively observable criteria. Hence, there is a need to understand under what conditions an application developed may fall under the category most heavily regulated, namely that of h-AIS. Thirdly, it will discuss the obligations that arise on developers and deployers of such systems already from the design phase, highlighting the need for a by-design approach in regulatory compliance, which requires keeping a lawyer-in-the-loop from the beginning of the conception of a technologically advanced system.
